◆ FluxForm

enum FluxForm

How the flux between two voxels is discretised.

The two differ exactly when the mobility varies in space, and the difference decides where the dye sits at equilibrium.

Enumerator
FLUX_SMOLUCHOWSKI 

: equilibrium uniform, independent of D.

Equilibrium is thermodynamics and mobility is kinetics, so a dye slowed by friction with no attraction is still found everywhere it can reach. This is the structure the Haas-Steinberg equation is written in, so that its stationary state is the given p(r) for any D.

FLUX_ITO 

: equilibrium .

Inherited from ChiSurf. It makes a friction field act as an attractive potential – halving the local mobility doubles the local population – which turned slow_factor into a disguised attraction to the quenchers. Kept so the old behaviour can be reproduced and compared.

Propagate many steps, reporting the surviving population periodically.

The loop belongs here, not in Python.** Calling diffusion_step() from a Python loop crosses the binding once per step and allocates a fresh grid each time; a solve is 1000-10000 steps, and doing that made the quenching suite forty times slower than the numba it replaced. Kept inside, the boundary is crossed once per solve and the buffers are reused.

[in]n_outreport the population every this many steps
[out]out_fluorescence,n_out_fluorescencethe surviving population at each reported step – n_steps / n_out + 1 of them
[out]out_view,n_out_viewthe final density

Both outputs are managed numpy views: the caller owns the buffers, and in C++ frees them with std::free. Not std::vector<double>& out-parameters – SWIG wraps std::vector<double> once across a module and its imports, so with rmf (and with it isd and saxs) in the dependencies the class a Python caller would have to construct is IMP.saxs.DistBase. Two views give a 2-tuple of arrays instead.

Adjoint of diffusion_propagate(): the gradient of a loss on the fluorescence.

For , with the populations diffusion_propagate() reports for the same cur, d, decay, bounds, ng, flux_form, n_steps, n_out, returns , and – every voxel at once, for the cost of about two forward propagations.

Why this is possible without a tape.** One sweep is linear in the density, , with built from d, decay and bounds only. So the adjoint density obeys (plus on every voxel at a reported step, since is a plain sum) – the transposed 7-point stencil, run backwards – and the parameter gradients are local products of the forward state and the adjoint at each step. The Smoluchowski flux is symmetric in the interface coefficient, so its transpose is the same stencil up to the bounds mask and where decay multiplies; the Ito flux is not, and both are written out.

Memory.** The backward sweep needs the forward state at every step and the forward keeps none. Storing all of them is doubles (2.6 GB at 4800 steps, ng = 41), so this checkpoints every steps and re-runs each segment forward on the way back: about twice the forward cost, two segments' worth of memory.

The gradients are of the discrete scheme actually run – exact for it, which is what makes them agree with a central difference of diffusion_propagate() to roundoff (the dot-product identity is the test). They are with respect to the folded coefficients d ( ) and decay ( ); the chain rule to and is the caller's, one multiplication each.

The same propagation, as one matrix exponential instead of n_steps sweeps.

diffusion_propagate() takes n_steps explicit Euler sweeps because forward Euler on a diffusion operator is stable only for . This takes none: the operator is constant over a run (d, decay and bounds do not change), so the whole propagation is for one , and it is symmetrisable (Smoluchowski is symmetric as it stands; Ito becomes so under ). A Lanczos projection onto krylov_dim vectors therefore answers every reported time at once.

Measured at 64-128 operator applications where the stepping route needs four thousand – 31-62x fewer – and more accurate, not less: diffusion_propagate() carries a forward-Euler and a first-order Lie splitting error of about 2e-6 that this does not. See okf/validation/larger_time_steps.md.

This does not reproduce diffusion_propagate()'s numbers.** It solves the semi-discrete equation; the other solves its own discretisation of it. The two agree to about the stepping scheme's own error, which is the honest tolerance for a comparison between them.

Parameters
[in]cur,d,decay,bounds,ng,flux_form,n_steps,n_outas diffusion_propagate()
[in]krylov_dimKrylov vectors to build; <= 0 selects 128. A smooth initial density needs 64, a point-like one 128. Cost is linear in it and the error falls superlinearly, so too large is cheap and too small is not.
[out]out_fluorescence,n_out_fluorescenceas diffusion_propagate()
[out]out_view,n_out_viewthe final density; pass nullptr for both to skip it and halve the work – the trace alone needs one pass, the density needs a second.
Exceptions
std::invalid_argumentif flux_form is FLUX_ITO and d is zero anywhere in the domain: the change of variables that symmetrises that form is .

diffusion_propagate_krylov() without the density: half the work.

The Krylov route needs one pass over the recursion for the trace and a second, with the coefficients then known, to rebuild the density – storing the basis instead would be 544 MB at ng = 81, m = 128. Calibration wants the trace and nothing else (every gradient in the quenching stack is a finite difference through one), so this is the entry that halves it.
